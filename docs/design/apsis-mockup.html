APSIS

A watch with no hands. A ring of minutes orbits off-centre inside a fixed circle and touches it at exactly one point. That point is the hour.

Browser mockup with sample weather and activity data. Drag the time to watch the ring travel through a day. Tap the centre of the dial to run Foresight.

How to read it

  1. Hour: where the circles touch. The white contact mark slides around the fixed circle once every 12 hours. The glowing crescent is always fattest directly opposite, so the whole dial leans toward the hour.
  2. Minute: the satellite on the ring. It circles the travelling ring once an hour, drags a short trail, and carries its own minute number so no counting is needed. The ring's ticks travel with it.
  3. Seconds: the moonlet. A small dot orbits the satellite once a minute. It is optional and rests on low battery.
  4. The next 8 hours: the corona. Each bar stands at the hour it describes. Height is temperature, colour runs cold to warm, a dot above means rain or snow. Bars fade the further ahead they are.
  5. The core. Weather now, the date, and a battery ring. It can show digital time or any complication instead.
  6. The rim. Four curved slots for whatever data the wearer wants, each with a progress line when the source has a goal.

The composition is never the same twice in a day: the ring swings around the core like a precessing orbit, so 3 o'clock and 9 o'clock look like different dials.

What is new here

Tangent time

Two circles and a point of contact replace the hands. The name comes from orbital mechanics: an apsis is the extreme point of an orbit, which is exactly where the minute ring meets the hour circle.

Hour marks that forecast

The hour markers are the weather for the hours they mark. Look at where 5 o'clock is and see what 5 o'clock will be like. Without forecast data they fall back to plain ticks.

Foresight

Tap the core and the dial fast-forwards: the contact point runs ahead through the next 8 hours while the core reads out each hour's temperature and conditions, then it glides back to now. Six seconds, on demand only.

Always-on and power

  • Always-on keeps only the two circles as hairlines, the contact mark, the satellite with its minute number, and the date. The crescent glow, corona, rim and moonlet are off, so very few pixels are lit.
  • Everything is drawn as vectors that rotate. Nothing animates continuously: the dial changes once a minute, plus the moonlet once a second when it is on.
  • The moonlet hides at 15% battery and in power saving. Foresight is disabled at 15%.

Build plan

  1. Scaffold. New face name and application ID, bundled Oxanium font, new strings, and an updated build script. Add the new ID to the companion's allowed watch faces.
  2. Time core. Fixed circle, crescent, travelling ring, contact mark, satellite, trail, minute number, moonlet. Validate and install on the watch to confirm legibility before anything else.
  3. Intrinsic data. Core weather and date, battery ring, corona with its fallback ticks.
  4. Complications. Four rim slots and the core slot, each handling goal, range, text and empty data.
  5. Foresight. Wire the companion tap trigger to the fast-forward and the hourly readout.
  6. Always-on, themes, power rules. Three colour themes, ambient scene, battery and power-saving cutoffs.
  7. Checks and documents. Expression and geometry tests, validator and memory checks, picker preview, README and data docs, device test on the Watch6 Classic.

Open risks to settle on the watch

  • Hourly forecast availability. The format documents up to 8 hours, but only current weather has been seen on the Watch6 so far. If hourly data is missing the corona shows ticks and Foresight reads only the current conditions.
  • Minute legibility. Reading an angle on an off-centre ring is unfamiliar. The minute number on the satellite is the safeguard, and digital time in the core is the fallback.
  • Curved slots in the Samsung editor. Arc-shaped tap targets and curved text need checking in the on-watch editor.
